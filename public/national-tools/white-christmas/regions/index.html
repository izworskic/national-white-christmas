Explore the U.S. snow regions

These regions are organized around the weather processes that actually create and preserve Christmas snow: lake effect, cold continental air, elevation, Pacific mountain storms, Atlantic coastal systems and persistent Arctic cold. They are designed as useful regional guides rather than thin state-by-state doorway pages.

Northeast

Northeast & New England

Interior New England, northern New York and higher terrain carry some of the East's strongest historical snow retention.

 Lake effect

Great Lakes & Ohio Valley

Lake-effect belts can build deep snow while nearby cities remain marginal, making local placement especially important.

 Continental cold

Upper Midwest & Northern Plains

Cold air and durable snowpack often matter more here than elevation or coastal storm tracks.

 Elevation

Rockies & Mountain West

Mountain elevation creates enormous local differences between high-country communities and nearby lower valleys.

 Pacific mountains

Pacific Northwest & Sierra

Cascades, Olympics and Sierra snow country can be very favorable while major lowland metros stay much less snowy.

 High desert & mountains

Southwest High Country

Flagstaff, northern New Mexico and other elevated areas can have meaningful Christmas snow despite southern latitude.

 Marginal snow belt

Mid-Atlantic & Appalachians

Elevation and storm track make the Appalachians substantially snowier than nearby coastal and Piedmont cities.

 Arctic & subarctic

Alaska

Much of Alaska operates in a completely different winter regime, with long-lived snowpack and extreme regional variation.

 Rare-snow edge

Southern Plains & Rare-Snow South

White Christmases are uncommon but not impossible from the Texas Panhandle through Oklahoma, Tennessee and the southern Appalachians.
